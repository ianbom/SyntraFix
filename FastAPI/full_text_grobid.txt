			<abstract>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><p>EEPIS Robot Soccer On Wheeled (ERSOW) is a wheeled soccer robot team competing in the Indonesian Wheeled Soccer Robot Contest. The ERSOW goalkeeper robot currently faces a challenge in responding to incoming balls due to delays in image processing, leading to slow movement responses. This delay can be mitigated by implementing a prediction system. By predicting the ball's trajectory, the robot can make timely movements to block shots effectively. This research designs a prediction system using the ZED stereo camera to enhance the goalkeeper robot's responsiveness in preventing goals. The research identifies the ball's position during airborne and ground states using the ZED camera. RGB-D readings are processed with Deep Learning methods to train on ball data and extract 3D object information from the dataset. The ball detection system is tested in three scenarios: right, center, and left positions relative to the robot. Accuracy evaluation is conducted three times for ball coordinates on the X, Y, and Z axes, resulting in X-, Y-, and Z-axis accuracy values of 90%, 80.77%, and 100%, respectively, with a ≤ 5 cm error tolerance. These results allow accurate determination of the ball's X, Y, and Z positions. The ball position data is then trained using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) to predict the ball's arrival position for the goalkeeper robot. Evaluation results show an overall accuracy of 70%, with an F1-score of 76% for the left position, 39% for the center position, and 84% for the right position.</p></div>

<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>I. INTRODUCTION</head><p>EEPIS Robot Soccer On Wheeled (ERSOW) is one of the robotics teams at Politeknik Elektronika Negeri Surabaya. In addition, the ERSOW team participated in an international level competition in the field of robotics, the Middle Size League Robot Soccer, initiated by the Robocup Federation <ref type="bibr" target="#b0">[1]</ref>, <ref type="bibr" target="#b1">[2]</ref>. The goal of these events, which center on wheeled robot soccer matches, is to build a robot soccer squad that can play independently and compete against the 2050 world champion human soccer team. In the research, The associate editor coordinating the review of this manuscript and approving it for publication was Yongming Li .</p><p>this soccer robot has implemented an autonomous system that requires the robot to be able to adapt to the surrounding environment and coordinate with other robots without any human intervention <ref type="bibr" target="#b2">[3]</ref>. This research is a continuation of previous research, namely creating a ball position estimation system with a neural network <ref type="bibr" target="#b3">[4]</ref>, creating a wheeled soccer robot navigation system and creating a ball position prediction system based on a stereo minor camera and neural network <ref type="bibr" target="#b4">[5]</ref>. There are many research topics on wheeled soccer robots developed such as in the field of computer vision, namely ball detection <ref type="bibr" target="#b5">[6]</ref>, <ref type="bibr" target="#b6">[7]</ref>, <ref type="bibr" target="#b7">[8]</ref> goal detection <ref type="bibr" target="#b8">[9]</ref>, <ref type="bibr" target="#b9">[10]</ref>, detection of enemy robots and friend robots <ref type="bibr" target="#b10">[11]</ref>, and its localization <ref type="bibr" target="#b11">[12]</ref>, <ref type="bibr" target="#b12">[13]</ref>. The ERSOW robot has been created with a number of basic soccer skills, such as finding the ball, dribbling the ball, avoiding robots from opponents, passing the ball to robot friends, timing to kick the ball into the goal, and so on. ERSOW has several robots, each of which has its own task, such as an attacking robot whose job is to score goals and contribute to the team's attack, a midfielder robot whose job is to help the team's defense, which has the ability to take the ball from the opponent and ward off the opponent's attack, and a goalkeeper robot whose job is to guard the team's goal from the opponent's attack and prevent goals from happening. The goalkeeper robot must have fast reflexes, good reaction speed, and the ability to ward off the ball with high accuracy. This research was conducted to improve the robot's ability to detect and predict the position of the ball. Better robot capabilities can increase the chances of winning in the competition. Predicting the position of the ball is needed so that the goalkeeper robot's ability can run optimally. With ball prediction, the goalkeeper robot will know the position of the direction of the ball's movement. The current problem is that the ERSOW goalkeeper robot still has obstacles in the robot's vision in detecting the ball with extreme light changes that can affect the ball prediction data during a soccer match. Previous research on object detection with Zed cameras has been conducted to see the performance and perspective of the camera, the effect of the camera polarizer and the measurement of the depth of the camera's distance from the object <ref type="bibr" target="#b13">[14]</ref>. Research on estimating the distance between the camera and the object has also been carried out using a monocular camera <ref type="bibr" target="#b14">[15]</ref>. Based on previous research, the ERSOW team wanted to create and implement a ball position detection system using the ZED camera because the camera has several advantages, namely, the image processing stage can be done inside the camera itself. The system created in this study is needed to increase the ability to detect the ball with the ability to detect the ball with changing lighting changes during the match. The detection ability is equipped with the ability to predict the position of the ball so as not to lose information. The contributions of this research are as follows:</p><p>• Proposing a system for predicting the position of the ball using the ZED camera because the ZED camera has the ability to detect ball objects with extreme lighting conditions on the Goalkeeper Robot Application.</p><p>• Proposing a system for predicting the ball's arrival position relative to the robot keeper using CNN. By accurately predicting the ball's trajectory, the robot keeper can better position itself to block incoming balls, thereby increasing its defensive capabilities. CNN was chosen because it can learn features automatically and is easy to integrate with low computing specifications in real-time. The best system performance is obtained by comparing several variations of the CNN method.</p><p>The following sections make up the next section of this paper: The design and use of the wheeled goalkeeping robot created by the ERSOW team is covered in general in Section II. Section III presents the system design and development of the proposed method in the form of ball object detection and ball position prediction. The experiments to collect the datasets and the processing techniques are presented in Section IV. Furthermore, Section IV also contains the results of the object detection and ball position prediction experiments which are then summarized in the conclusion in Section V.</p></div>

<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>III. HARDWARE AND SOFTWARE ARCHITECTURE</head><p>The process of system completion involves multiple stages that are intended to accomplish the objectives of the research.</p><p>Every phase is meticulously organized to guarantee that every facet of the investigation is appropriately handled and carried out. When the ZED camera on the goalkeeper robot detects the ball, the first step is to determine whether the ball is detected or not. If the ball is not detected, the process of capturing images and analysis will be repeated continuously until the ball is successfully detected by the camera. Once the ball is detected, information about the ball's position is sent to the goalkeeper robot for further processing. Fig. <ref type="figure" target="#fig_0">1</ref> shows the overall robot system where the camera is useful for obtaining information about the ball on the field. At this stage, the goalkeeper robot will examine the information it receives from the ZED camera using a prediction algorithm. This algorithm aims to estimate the ball's position based on the images and data captured by the camera. Machine learning techniques, in particular Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), are used in the prediction process. These networks have been trained with a dataset that contains a variety of probable ball placements.</p><p>The predicted ball position is then classified into one of three main categories: left, center, or right. This classification helps the robot understand more specifically where the ball is likely to move. Consequently, the goalkeeper robot can respond more quickly and accurately to block the ball. Based on this prediction, the robot will take appropriate action, such as moving left, right, or staying in the center, to block the ball and prevent a goal. This process of detecting, analyzing, and predicting the ball's position is a crucial part of the goalkeeper robot's defense system in robot soccer. By utilizing the ZED camera technology and advanced machine learning algorithms, this system is designed to enhance the speed and accuracy of the robot's response to the ball's movement, thereby increasing the chances of successfully blocking goals from the opposing team.</p><p>A. HARDWARE ARCHITECTURE Fig. <ref type="figure" target="#fig_1">2</ref> shows the hardware architecture of the ERSOW team goalkeeper robot. This architecture includes key components such as the ZED 2i Camera and mini-PC. The ZED 2i Camera serves as the robot's vision system, providing the visual data required for navigation and object detection. The mini-PC acts as the brain of the system, effectively analyzing the information from the camera and directing the robot's motions. The combination of these two components allows the robot goalkeeper to operate with high precision and reliability in robotic soccer matches. The ZED Stereo camera, manufactured by Stereolabs, is the first sensor to offer depth awareness both indoors and outdoors across larger distances (up to 20 meters). The ZED 2i Stereo camera is a device that attempts to replicate human vision. Due to the approximately 65mm horizontal separation between human eyes, each eye sees a situation slightly differently. Using two lenses can provide 3D information of an observed scene, allowing developers to add 3D depth information to robotics navigation, skeleton tracking, object detection, position tracking and mapping, and other applications. Via a USB 3.0 interface, the output data is high-resolution side-by-side color video. The ZED program on the host computer uses the two synchronized left and right video streams in this movie to create a depth map of the scene. The camera records a distance value (Z, in metric units) for every pixel (X, Y) in the picture, measured from the left lens's back to the scene object, and saves it in the depth map. Fig. <ref type="figure" target="#fig_2">3</ref> shows that the depth map captured by the ZED camera stores a distance value (Z) for each pixel (X, Y) in the image. These distance values are expressed in metric units (e.g. meters) and are calculated from the back of the camera's left eye to the objects in the scene. Such depth maps cannot be displayed directly because they are encoded in a 32-bit format.</p><p>In order to display this depth map, an 8-bit monochrome (grayscale) representation with values between 0 and 255 is required, where a value of 255 represents the closest possible depth distance and a value of 0 represents the furthest possible depth distance.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>B. SOFTWARE ARCHITECTURE</head><p>This research uses the ROS system because it has the ability to integrate camera sensors with artificial intelligence methods and several other combined sensors in the robot. Fig. <ref type="figure" target="#fig_3">4</ref> shows the basic communication system for accessing the ZED camera on the robot. Image information is acquired over time and processed in real-time.  The prediction system that has been created using the CNN algorithm tries to change the layer parameter model to get the best results in classifying the position of the predicted direction of the ball. The dataset was collected as shown in Fig. <ref type="figure" target="#fig_5">6</ref>. The CNN design used is as in Fig. <ref type="figure" target="#fig_6">7</ref> and equations ( <ref type="formula" target="#formula_2">4</ref>) to <ref type="bibr" target="#b8">(9)</ref>. Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is a type of artificial neural network specifically designed to process two-dimensional grid-shaped data, such as images. The convolution layer, pooling layer, and fully connected layer are the three primary hierarchical layers of a CNN. The central component of a CNN is the convolution layer, where different visual elements like edges, textures, and patterns are detected by applying filters or kernels to the input. A feature map reflecting the position of a certain feature in the original image is produced by the convolution layer's filters scanning the entire input. The way that neurons in the human brain react to visual stimuli is comparable to this mechanism.</p><p>The pooling layer, which comes after the convolution layer, is meant to combine key information and lower the dimensionality of the feature map. Max pooling, which chooses the maximum value within each condensed area of the feature map, is a frequently used kind of pooling. This lessens the number of parameters that must be computed and increases the model's resistance to slight variations in the input features' locations.</p><p>CNNs typically have multiple sets of successive convolution and pooling layers, which allow the network to detect increasingly complex features at each layer. Early layers may detect simple edges and lines, while deeper layers may detect more complex objects, such as specific shapes and patterns.</p><p>The final input is supplied into the fully connected layer after passing through several convolution and pooling layers. Every neuron in this layer is connected to every other neuron in the layer before it, enabling the network to compile the features that were taken out of the earlier levels and come to a conclusion. An activation function that aids in the final classification, such as ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) or Softmax, typically follows this completely connected layer.</p><p>This study designs and implements appropriate parameter values in each layer of the CNN used. Previous researchers have created several methods for artificial intelligence that have been tried to be implemented on the ERSOW robot. These methods are Label Propagation, Extra Tree Classifier, Ridge Classifier, Linear SVC, Passive Aggressive Classifier, AdaBoost, Label Spreading, Nearest Centroid, GaussianNB, and Dummy Classifier</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>IV. METHODOLOGY</head><p>The diagram of the dataset creation and ball position prediction process illustrates the sequential steps involved in capturing images from the goalkeeper robot's camera, labeling images, creating a dataset directory, and training the YOLO model. The process continues with activating the camera, loading the deep learning model, reading RGB-depth images, extracting coordinates, and collecting ball position data. The dataset is then created and used to train the CNN model, which predicts ball positions, leading to the final prediction output.</p><p>The process begins with image capture using a ZED 2i camera mounted on the robot goalkeeper. These images are then stored in the dataset directory for the labeling process. The labeling process is done to mark the position of the ball in each image, which is then used to train the object detection model using YOLO (You Only Look Once).</p><p>Once the object detection model is trained, it is loaded into the system for the ball detection process. The system uses RGB images and depth information from the ZED 2i camera to detect and identify the position of the ball in X, Y, and Z coordinates. This information is then extracted from the images processed by the system.</p><p>With the camera active, the system continuously reads the position of the ball and performs detection using the trained deep learning model. The detected ball position data is then collected and stored for use in the ball prediction training process.</p><p>The ball prediction training process is performed using a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). The ball position data that has been collected from the detection process is used to train the ball prediction model. The purpose of this training is to generate a model that can estimate the ball's arrival position based on previous position data, allowing the robot goalkeeper to react faster and more accurately.</p><p>Once the ball prediction model is trained, it is implemented in the robot goalkeeper system. The system was then tested to evaluate the performance of ball position detection and prediction. The tests were conducted with three different scenarios: when the ball is on the right, center, and left side of the robot.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>A. LIMITATIONS OF THE METHOD</head><p>While the methodology employed in this study shows promising results in improving the accuracy of ball position prediction, several limitations need to be addressed:</p><p>Lighting Sensitivity: The ZED 2i camera and the CNN-based model show sensitivity to extreme lighting conditions, such as very bright or low-light environments. Under these conditions, the quality of image data is reduced, which can negatively impact the accuracy of ball detection and position prediction.</p><p>High-Speed Ball Detection: The current model struggles to maintain accuracy when the ball is moving at high speeds. This indicates that the dataset used for training might not fully represent the range of ball speeds encountered in real soccer scenarios, leading to less reliable predictions in high-velocity situations.</p><p>Overfitting in Training: During the training phase, overfitting was observed, particularly between epoch 41 and 60. Although the model performs well on the training data, its performance on unseen validation data fluctuated, indicating that the model may have learned patterns specific to the training set but may not generalize well to new data.</p><p>Camera Dependence: The system is heavily reliant on the quality of RGB and depth data from the ZED 2i camera. Any issues with camera calibration or environmental interference could degrade the accuracy of the position prediction. Furthermore, the system's performance could be impacted by noise or limitations in the depth measurement at certain distances.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>1) BALL DETECTION TRAINING a: CAPTURE IMAGE FROM CAMERA OF THE GOALKEEPER ROBOT</head><p>The image data capture process was carried out using ZED 2i cameras mounted on the center and front sides of the robot goalkeeper. This strategic placement ensures optimal visual coverage of the pitch and ball, allowing the camera to record information with high accuracy. This camera position is important to ensure the image data covers various aspects of the game, both when the ball is on the field and in the air.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>b: LABEL THE IMAGE</head><p>Once the image is captured, the next step is to label the image. Image labeling is done by marking the object to be detected, in this case, the ball. This process is important to provide the data required by the model to learn to recognize objects.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>c: CREATE DATASET</head><p>This dataset will be fed into the object detection model in order to train it. In order for the model to detect objects in various situations, the dataset should include various positions and lighting conditions. This includes variations in the camera's angle of view, the distance between the camera and the object, and the intensity and direction of light. By including images in bright, dim, shadowed, and shadowless conditions, the model will be more robust and able to recognize objects with high accuracy in diverse realworld conditions. This variation ensures that the model does not rely solely on specific visual patterns, but can generalize to new situations that may be encountered during actual operations.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>d: TRAIN YOLO MODEL</head><p>At this stage, the YOLOv8 (You Only Look Once version 8) model is trained using the prepared dataset. YOLOv8 is the latest version of an object detection method that is renowned for its efficiency and speed. In the training process, the model will learn to recognize and detect various objects in pre-labeled images. This training involves optimizing a loss function designed to minimize the difference between the model's predictions and the ground truth labels. The loss function in YOLOv8 takes into account errors in prediction of bounding box position, bounding box size, and object classification. The training process is performed using backpropagation and stochastic gradient descent techniques to update the model weights until it reaches the desired level of accuracy. The trained model is then evaluated to ensure high object detection performance on previously unseen test images. The innovations in YOLOv8 enable improved detection accuracy and speed, making it a highly effective tool in various object recognition applications.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>2) BALL DETECTION a: ACTIVE THE CAMERA</head><p>The ball detection process begins by activating the camera attached to the robot goalkeeper. This camera is tasked with capturing images and videos of the robot's surroundings with high resolution and sufficient image capture rate for real-time applications. Camera activation is an important first step to ensure that the system can acquire the visual data required for the subsequent detection and analysis steps.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>b: LOAD THE MODEL</head><p>Once the camera is activated, a pre-trained ball detection model using the YOLO (You Only Look Once) algorithm is loaded into the system. The YOLO model is designed to analyze images and videos captured by the camera efficiently and quickly. YOLO works by dividing images into grids and processing them in a single stage to detect objects, including balls, with high accuracy. This process involves extracting visual features from the image which are then processed by the YOLO model to recognize patterns corresponding to the characteristics of the ball. The use of YOLO enables object detection in real-time, which is crucial for robotic applications that require quick responses.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>c: READ RGB &amp; DEPTH IMAGES</head><p>The camera then captures images in RGB (Red, Green, Blue) format that provide rich color information as well as depth images that provide information about the distance of each pixel in the image from the camera. Depth images are very important as they allow the system to determine the distance of the object from the camera with high accuracy, which is critical information for figuring out the three-dimensional (3D) position of the ball. The combination of RGB and depth data allows the system to not only detect the ball but also measure its distance precisely, which is essential for trajectory calculation and ball motion prediction.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>d: EXTRACT COORDINATES (X, Y, Z)</head><p>Using the RGB image and depth, the system then extracts the coordinates of the ball in three dimensions (x, y, z). These coordinates indicate the position of the sphere relative to the camera. The formula for calculating the depth z in real-world coordinates can be expressed as equations (1) to <ref type="bibr" target="#b2">(3)</ref>.</p><formula xml:id="formula_0">z = f × B d (1)</formula><p>where:</p><p>• f is the focal length of the camera,</p><p>• B is the distance between the two camera lenses(baseline),</p><p>• d is the horizontal shift between the left and right images (disparity)</p><p>The formula for calculating the coordinates x and y in the real world are:</p><formula xml:id="formula_1">x = (u -c x ) × z f z (2) y = (v -c y ) × z f y (3)</formula><p>where:</p><p>• u and v are the coordinates of pixels in the image,</p><p>• c x and c y is the coordinate of the image center,</p><p>• f x and f y is the focal length in pixel units.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>3) BALL ARRIVAL PREDICTION TRAINING a: COLLECT DATA</head><p>The dataset creation process starts with data capture, where the robot goalkeeper is placed in the goal area, and the ball is kicked from three different directions. Data was recorded using a ZED camera to learn various ball movement patterns and match situations. Capturing data from different angles and conditions ensures the accuracy of the ball movement prediction by the system, as shown in Fig. <ref type="figure" target="#fig_5">6</ref>. The process of capturing and processing data to predict the position of the ball in the goal area. Sample data is taken from three initial positions of the ball, namely (x1, y1), (x2, y1), and (x3, y1), and rolled to three waypoints: left, center, and right, with sampling every 20 milliseconds. The ball's routes as it moved from different spots on the field toward the goal were tracked and analyzed to forecast its next location, which was divided into three categories: left, center, and right.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>b: CREATE DATASET</head><p>The ball position data that has been collected is then processed and made into a structured dataset. This dataset will be used as input to train the ball prediction model. To ensure the model can make predictions with high accuracy, the dataset should include various scenarios, such as ball movement at different speeds, different shooting angles, and varying environmental conditions. The data also needs to be tagged with time information to enable dynamic analysis of the ball movement. Dataset creation involves a process of data cleaning, tagging, and splitting the data into training and testing sets, with a total of 450 training data points and 180 testing data points, to ensure the resulting model can generalize to new situations that may be encountered during a match. Variation in the dataset is essential to improve the model's ability to predict ball position accurately and responsively.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>c: TRAINING CNN MODEL</head><p>The Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model is trained using the prepared dataset. CNN is a type of deep neural network that is highly effective for the analysis and processing of visual data such as images and videos. In this context, CNN is used to predict the position of the ball based on the position data that has been collected during the training session. The training process involves adjusting the weights of the model through the backpropagation technique to minimize the prediction error. The Fig. <ref type="figure" target="#fig_6">7</ref> is a sequential model of the CNN architecture for ball position prediction. A sequential model is a series of layers that are processed sequentially from input to output. The given model consists of several types of layers: Conv1D, AveragePooling1D, Dropout, MaxPooling1D, Flatten, and Dense. The operation process is in equations ( <ref type="formula" target="#formula_2">4</ref>) to <ref type="bibr" target="#b8">(9)</ref>.</p><p>1. Conv1D Layer</p><p>• Function: Filtering the input signal using a filter.</p><p>• Operation:</p><formula xml:id="formula_2">y (t) = (x * w) (t) + b (<label>4</label></formula><formula xml:id="formula_3">)</formula><p>where y is the input, w is the filter weight, b is the bias, and * is the convolution operation.</p><p>• Output: Filtered signal.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head n="2.">AveragePooling1D layer</head><p>• Function: Reduces the dimensionality of the signal by averaging the values within a specific window.</p><p>• Operation:</p><formula xml:id="formula_4">y (t) = 1 k k-1 i=0 x(t + i) (<label>5</label></formula><formula xml:id="formula_5">)</formula><p>where y is pooling window size.</p><p>• Output: A signal whose size is smaller than the average value of the pooling window.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head n="3.">Dropout layer</head><p>• Function: Prevent overfitting by randomly disabling some units (neurons) during training.</p><p>• Operation:</p><formula xml:id="formula_6">y (t) = x (t) .m(t)<label>(6)</label></formula><p>where m(t) is a mask containing zero or one, determined randomly.</p><p>• Output: A signal for which some units are disabled.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head n="4.">MaxPooling1D layer</head><p>• Function: Reduces the dimensionality of the signal by taking the maximum value within a specific window.</p><p>• Operation:</p><formula xml:id="formula_7">y (t) = max(x (t) , x (t + 1) , . . . , x(t + k -1)) (<label>7</label></formula><formula xml:id="formula_8">)</formula><p>where k is the size of the pooling window.</p><p>• Output: A signal whose size is smaller than the maximum value of the pooling window.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head n="5.">Flatten layer</head><p>• Function: Converts multi-dimensional signals into onedimensional (1D) signals.</p><p>• Output: 1D signal. 6. Dense (Fully Connected) layer</p><p>• Function: Linearly combine all the signal features and apply an activation function.</p><p>• Operation:</p><formula xml:id="formula_9">y = σ (Wx + b) (<label>8</label></formula><formula xml:id="formula_10">)</formula><p>where x is the input, σ is the activation function, w is the weight matrix, and b is the bias vector.</p><p>• Output: The signal that has been transformed by the dense layer.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>i) TRANSFORMATION STEPS IN THE MODEL</head><p>Here is the complete sequence of transformations that occur to the input signal as it passes through each layer in the model: 1. Conv1D 1:</p><p>• Input: (None, 10, 512)</p><p>• Output: (None, 10, 512)</p><p>• Convolution operation with a certain filter size. 2. AveragePooling1D 1:</p><p>• Input: (None, 10, 512)</p><p>• Output: (None, 5, 512)</p><p>• Takes the average of every two consecutive values, halving the length of the signal. The model takes an input signal, operates it through multiple layers of convolution, pooling, dropout, and finally dense layers to produce the output. Each layer has specific functions that help in extracting important features from the signal and finally make predictions based on those features.</p><p>To train the CNN, a loss function is used which measures the difference between the model's prediction and the true value. The loss function used is the Mean Squared Error (MSE), which can be expressed by the following formula:</p><formula xml:id="formula_11">Loss = 1 N N i=1 ŷi -y i 2<label>(9)</label></formula><p>where:</p><p>• ŷi is the model prediction for the i-th sample,</p><p>• y i is the actual value for the i-th sample,</p><p>• N is total number of samples in the dataset.</p><p>During the training phase, the model weights are updated using the average square of the difference between the predicted and actual values, which is computed using this loss function. By minimizing this loss value, the CNN model is expected to improve its prediction accuracy of the ball position.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>4) BALL ARRIVAL PREDICTION a: INPUT BALL POSITION</head><p>The system is input into the prediction model. This position includes the coordinates of the moving ball. This data is obtained from cameras and sensors that monitor the ball's movement in real-time. This coordinate information is then used by the prediction model to calculate the ball's next trajectory.</p></div>
<div xmlns="http://www.tei-c.org/ns/1.0"><head>b: POSITION PREDICTION</head><p>The prediction model then analyzes the ball position input and predicts the future direction and position of the ball. This prediction helps the robot goalkeeper determine the movements to be made to prevent the ball from entering the goal. The position prediction can be expressed by equation <ref type="bibr" target="#b9">(10)</ref>.</p><formula xml:id="formula_12">p (t) = f (p (t -1) , p (t -2) , . . . , p(t -n)) (<label>10</label></formula><formula xml:id="formula_13">)</formula><p>where:</p><p>• p(t) is the predicted position of the ball at time t,</p><p>• p (t -1) , p (t -2) , . . . , p(tn) is the position of the ball at the previous time, which is used as input for the prediction function.</p></div>

This information is used by the goalkeeping robot control system to take appropriate actions in blocking the ball. This output allows the robot to respond quickly and accurately to the movement of the ball, optimizing the chances of stopping a shot from entering the goal.
